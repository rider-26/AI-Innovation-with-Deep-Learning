02 · EDA: interactions between features (Jadon, wide-and-deep)¶

Why this notebook exists. My model is wide-and-deep. Its wide part is a linear model on crossed features: one weight for each combination, such as "Gold customer AND last order 31-60 days ago". A cross is only worth adding if the combination tells us something that the two columns do not tell us on their own. That is called an interaction.

The question. Does the effect of one feature depend on the value of another? For example: does recency matter more for Gold than for Bronze customers? If it does, a customer_tier x recency cross lets the wide part learn a separate rule for each tier.

What this notebook decides. Which crosses go into the wide part. They are saved to results/wide_deep_crosses_jadon.json, and wide_deepMODEL.ipynb reads them from there.

Section What it does
0 Setup, data, shared split
1 The headline question: recency x tier
2 A screen of every candidate pair, with a statistical test
3 A closer look at the strongest crosses, cell by cell
4 Decisions for the model

Only the train split is explored. If I chose crosses by looking at validation or test customers, the model's scores on them would be flattered. Validation is used once, in Section 2, the same way it is used for tuning: to check that a pattern still holds on unseen customers.

How to read the chart. If recency mattered equally for every tier, the lines would be parallel (each tier shifted up or down by the same amount). A line that drops more steeply means recency matters more for that tier. Lines that are not parallel are an interaction, and a cross can capture it.

The cell below puts a number on "steeper": for each tier, the buy rate of recent customers (0-30 days) divided by the rate of lapsed customers (181-365 days).

So yes: recency matters more for Gold than for Bronze (27 vs 11 points). The lines are clearly not parallel. At 0-30 days the error bars of the four tiers do not overlap, but by 181-365 days all tiers have converged to about 10-12%: a high tier is only worth something while the customer is still active. Every customer whose last order was over 365 days ago, or who never ordered, is Bronze (about 22,000 of the 28,600 Bronze customers in train), which is why Bronze has the flattest line.

2. A screen of every candidate pair¶

Section 1 looked at one pair because the business asked about it. Many other pairs could interact too, so they are all tested in the same way.

Candidates.

  • Text columns with few levels: country, tier, region tier, channel, language. region is left out: 100 regions x 5 buckets = 500 cells, most of them too small to learn a rule from (Leon's embedding model handles region instead).
  • Numeric columns: days_since_last_order plus the 5 numbers most related to buying on the train split (by Spearman correlation). Each is cut into 5 equal-sized buckets (quantiles).

The test, for each pair (a, b) (interaction_test in src/wide_deep.py). Two logistic regressions on the bucketed columns:

Model Formula Meaning
main effects buy ~ a + b each column adds its own amount
with cross buy ~ a + b + a x b every combination can have its own rate
  • Deviance gain and p-value (likelihood-ratio test on train). With 44,800 customers almost any pattern is "significant", so the p-value alone is too easy to pass.
  • Validation log-loss gain. The practical test: does the cross still make predictions better for customers it was not fitted on? Above 0 = it helps. This is the number used to rank pairs.

3. A closer look at the strongest crosses¶

A cross can win the screen because of one tiny cell with an extreme rate. Before trusting it, look at every cell of the top three: the buy rate, and the number of customers behind it.

In [9]:
from wide_deep import Bucketizer

def cross_table(col_a, col_b):
    # Buy rate and customer count for every combination of the two (bucketed) columns.
    bk = Bucketizer().fit(X_train, [col_a, col_b])
    b = bk.transform(X_train)
    t = pd.DataFrame({"a": b[col_a], "b": b[col_b], "bought": y_train})
    rates = t.pivot_table(index="a", columns="b", values="bought", aggfunc="mean")
    sizes = t.pivot_table(index="a", columns="b", values="bought", aggfunc="size")

    def order(col, present):
        # Low -> high for numbers (bucketizer order), Bronze -> Platinum for tiers.
        wanted = TIER_ORDER if col == "customer_tier" else bk.order_.get(col, sorted(present))
        return [v for v in wanted if v in present] + sorted(set(present) - set(wanted))

    rows, cols = order(col_a, rates.index), order(col_b, rates.columns)
    return rates.loc[rows, cols], sizes.loc[rows, cols]

4. Decisions for the model¶

Rule. Keep a cross if (1) it improves validation log loss (gain above 0) and (2) its likelihood-ratio p-value is below 0.001. Keep at most 8, best first: each cross adds tens of weights, and the wide part should stay small enough to read. customer_tier x days_since_last_order is the headline question, so it is reported whether or not it is kept.

Findings¶

  1. Recency x tier. Recency matters more for higher tiers: recent customers buy 3.2x as often as lapsed ones for Gold, against 2.4x for Bronze (5.2x for Platinum). The screen confirms it is not chance (p = 8e-17, validation log-loss gain +0.63 x 1e-3), and the cross is kept (6th of 8).
  2. The strongest crosses all pair loyalty or recency with recent activity:
    • customer_tier x orders_90d: a Platinum customer with no order in the last 90 days buys only 21% of the time, against 58% with at least one. Tier without recent orders means little.
    • days_since_last_order x sessions_90d: lapsed customers (181-365 days) who still visited 5+ times in 90 days buy 28% of the time, against 4% for those who did not visit. Customers who are still browsing are the best win-back targets.
    • customer_tier x sessions_30d: the same story with visits in the last 30 days (Platinum 62% with 2+ visits against 19% with none).
  3. What was rejected and why. 22 of the 46 pairs made validation worse. 14 more improved validation a little but were not significant (p between 0.007 and 0.98), so their gain is most likely luck. Almost all of them involve country, channel, language or region, for example preferred_channel x sessions_90d (p = 0.91): where or how a customer shops does not change how recency and activity work. Two pairs passed both tests but were cut by the limit of 8 (orders_365d x orders_90d, sessions_90d x orders_90d); both repeat information the kept crosses already have.
  4. What this decides. 8 crosses go into the wide part of wide_deepMODEL.ipynb (results/wide_deep_crosses_jadon.json). Even the best one improves validation log loss by only about 0.001, so the interactions are real but small. That suggests the deep part may learn them on its own, which the ablation in the model notebook tests.
